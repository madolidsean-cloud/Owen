What Are Black Holes?

A black hole is a region of spacetime whose gravity is so strong that, once matter or light crosses its event horizon, it cannot get back out. The event horizon is a boundary, not a solid surface. Black holes are detected by observing how they affect nearby stars and gas, and by studying radiation from material falling toward them.

Key concept: A black hole itself does not shine like a star. However, gas spiraling around it can become extremely hot and bright before crossing the event horizon. That glowing material is one of the ways astronomers find and study black holes.

Black holes are predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity. Astronomers now have several kinds of evidence for them, including stellar orbits, X-rays from hot gas, gravitational waves from mergers, and images of the glowing surroundings of black holes.

Types of Black Holes

Stellar-mass black holes

These form when the cores of some massive stars collapse at the end of their lives. Their masses are several to many times the mass of the Sun; the exact outcome depends on the star and its surroundings.

Supermassive black holes

These contain millions or billions of solar masses and are found in the centers of many large galaxies. How the earliest supermassive black holes gained their first mass so quickly is an active research question.

Intermediate-mass candidates

Black holes between stellar-mass and supermassive sizes are difficult to find. A number of candidates have been reported, and astronomers are working to establish their masses and nature.

Primordial black holes

These are hypothetical black holes that may have formed from dense regions in the very early universe. They have not been confirmed.

How Black Holes Form

From the death of a massive star

A massive star spends much of its life balancing inward gravity with outward pressure from nuclear fusion. When its fuel is depleted, the core may no longer be able to support itself. It collapses, and the star's outer layers may be expelled in a supernova. If the remaining core is massive enough, it can continue collapsing into a black hole. Not every massive star makes a black hole; some leave neutron stars instead.

Possible routes to the first giant black holes

The first supermassive black holes had to grow in a young universe. One proposed route starts with ordinary stellar remnants that gain mass by pulling in gas and merging with other black holes. Another is a heavy seed: in special conditions, a large cloud of early, mostly hydrogen and helium gas may avoid breaking into many normal stars and collapse into a much more massive black-hole seed. This is called the direct-collapse scenario. It is a leading hypothesis, not a process JWST has watched happen conclusively.

Why the early universe is a puzzle: JWST has found candidate actively growing black holes in very distant galaxies. Because their light has traveled for billions of years, we see them as they were when the universe was young. Their apparent early presence motivates theories for rapid growth and massive starting seeds, but observations do not yet identify one single formation route for all of them.

JWST and the “Black-Hole Star” Idea

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observes infrared light, allowing it to study very distant galaxies whose light has been stretched by the expansion of the universe. Webb can take spectra: spreading an object's light into a rainbow of wavelengths so astronomers can examine features made by atoms and molecules. Those features help reveal the object's distance, gas, and energy sources.

What has Webb found?

Webb has identified compact, very distant objects nicknamed little red dots. In 2026, NASA reported that deep JWST spectroscopy of the lensed candidate GLIMPSE-17775 showed a rich set of emission-line features consistent with hot, dense gas around a rapidly feeding black hole. The spectrum offers the strongest evidence yet for the proposed “black-hole star” picture. The interpretation is based on the object's light and its spectral fingerprints—not a direct photograph of a black hole or of one forming.

Important caveat: “Strongest evidence yet” is not the same as final proof. Astronomers compare the observed spectrum with predictions and alternative explanations. These distant, faint objects are challenging to measure, so follow-up observations and analysis are needed to test the interpretation.

What does “black-hole star” mean?

In this context, it does not mean a normal star with a black hole inside it. It is a proposed, short-lived stage in which a growing black hole is wrapped in a thick envelope of gas. Gas falling inward releases energy; the surrounding material absorbs and re-emits some of that energy, making the whole unresolved object look compact and star-like from far away. The black hole powers the glow through accretion, rather than through ordinary stellar fusion.

How might one form?

  1. A dense, early-universe gas cloud collapses and may create a relatively massive black-hole seed.
  2. The seed sits in a gas-rich environment and pulls in surrounding material.
  3. Rapid accretion heats the infalling gas and produces intense radiation.
  4. A thick gas envelope can reprocess that light, giving the system a compact, star-like appearance.
  5. As the black hole grows or the surrounding gas is dispersed, this “black-hole star” phase would end.

Direct collapse and a black-hole star describe related but different ideas: direct collapse is a proposed way to make a massive seed; “black-hole star” describes a possible luminous, gas-enshrouded accretion phase. JWST's candidate evidence does not by itself prove how the seed originally formed.

Not the same as a dark star: In much of astrophysics, “dark star” refers to a hypothetical early star whose energy comes from dark-matter annihilation. A “black-hole star” in the JWST reports is instead a black hole accreting gas inside a dense envelope. The names sound similar, but the proposed objects and power sources are different.

Fascinating Facts About Black Holes

  • Black holes can be detected through their effects on nearby matter, even though the black hole itself emits no light from inside its event horizon.
  • Gravity affects the passage of time; clocks close to a black hole run differently compared with clocks far away.
  • The supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way is called Sagittarius A*.
  • The Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the first image of a black hole's shadow, in galaxy M87, in 2019.
  • Hawking radiation is a theoretical prediction that black holes can slowly lose energy; it has not been detected from an astrophysical black hole.
  • When black holes merge, they send ripples through spacetime called gravitational waves.
  • Hot gas in an accretion disk around a black hole can emit X-rays and other radiation.

How Scientists Study Black Holes

No single telescope can answer every question about black holes. Astronomers combine observations across the electromagnetic spectrum with gravitational-wave measurements and computer models.

Event Horizon Telescope (EHT): A planet-wide network of radio observatories that has produced images of the glowing surroundings and shadows of supermassive black holes.
Gravitational-wave observatories: LIGO, Virgo, and KAGRA measure ripples in spacetime from events such as black-hole mergers, providing information about the objects' masses and spins.
JWST: Webb's infrared imaging and spectroscopy help astronomers investigate distant galaxies and possible actively growing black holes from the early universe.

By comparing evidence from these different methods, scientists can test how black holes grow, how they affect their galaxies, and whether proposed early-universe objects are truly powered by black holes.
